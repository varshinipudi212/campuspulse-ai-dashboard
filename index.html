Executive proposal
Turn a one-hour AI workshop into a measurable campus growth experiment.
CampusPulse AI proposes a focused seven-day acquisition plan for the workshop concept “Build Your First AI Project in 60 Minutes”, aimed at final-year engineering students. The plan works backward from a target of 500 registrations and a proposed budget cap of ₹2,000, using trackable channels and fast learning loops rather than unmeasured reach.

Strategic choices

Audience: Final-year engineering students who want a practical, beginner-friendly introduction to building an AI project.

Channel focus: Campus ambassadors, relevant college clubs and organic student communities.

Success standard: Report unique registrations, conversion, attribution and cost—not impressions alone.

Seven-day execution plan

Each day produces a tangible output and a measurable learning objective.
DAY 1 · FOUNDATION

Instrument the funnel

Finalize the workshop value proposition, registration form, channel tags, referral codes and dashboard definitions.

Deliverable: Tested form and attribution-ready tracker.

DAY 2 · AMBASSADORS

Prepare campus distribution

Create concise share copy and a unique referral code for each participating ambassador.

Deliverable: Trackable ambassador assets.

DAY 3 · STUDENT COMMUNITIES

Activate club-led distribution

Prepare club-specific copy and separate source tags for relevant student communities.

Deliverable: Channel-level source visibility.

DAY 4 · EXPERIMENT

Test message clarity

Compare the specific “Build Your First AI Project in 60 Minutes” proposition with generic workshop wording across comparable audiences.

Primary metric: Registration conversion rate.

DAY 5 · ANALYZE

Compare quality, not just reach

Review unique registrations, duplicates, attribution, visitor-to-registration conversion and spend by channel.

Deliverable: Evidence-based channel comparison.

DAY 6 · OPTIMIZE

Shift effort where evidence supports it

Prioritize the strongest channel only when observed conversion and cost justify the change. Do not optimize from simulated values.

Guardrail: Remain within the ₹2,000 cap.

DAY 7 · DECIDE

Report outcomes and next steps

Compare verified unique registrations with the 500 target, summarize experiment learnings and document limitations.

Deliverable: Final dashboard and recommendation.

MEASUREMENT FRAMEWORK

Define success before launch

Primary KPI: Verified unique registrations. Diagnostic KPIs: conversion rate, source attribution, duplicates/invalid codes and cost per registration.

Decision rule: Keep targets, forecasts and actuals distinct.

Experiment design

01 · Referral attribution

Hypothesis: Unique ambassador codes will reveal which campus referrals produce registrations.

Test: Compare unique registrations attributed to valid codes.

Metric: Referred registrations and cost per registration.

02 · Value proposition

Hypothesis: A concrete 60-minute project outcome will outperform generic workshop wording.

Test: Compare two message variants across similar audiences.

Metric: Conversion rate by message variant.

03 · Distribution channel

Hypothesis: Relevant college clubs may deliver more efficient registrations than broad organic posting.

Test: Tag club and organic sources separately.

Metric: Unique registrations, conversion and cost per registration.

AI worklog: prompt → output → review → decision

Illustrative worklog examples documenting how AI can support planning. These are proposed examples for this submission, not claims of campaign execution or measured results.
EXAMPLE 01 · CHANNEL STRATEGY

Keep the plan measurable

Prompt: “Suggest low-cost ways to reach 500 student registrations in seven days with a ₹2,000 cap. Include ways to measure each channel.”

AI output: Try campus ambassadors, college clubs, social posts, paid ads, influencer partnerships and email outreach.

Human review: Six channels would spread a small budget and short timeline too thin; paid or influencer reach may also be difficult to verify.

Final decision: Prioritize ambassadors, relevant college clubs and organic student communities, each with source tags or referral codes. Treat the 500 registrations as a target, not a forecast.

EXAMPLE 02 · MESSAGE TEST

Lead with a concrete outcome

Prompt: “Draft two concise workshop messages for final-year engineering students: one outcome-led and one generic. Avoid unsupported promises.”

AI output: Variant A: “Build Your First AI Project in 60 Minutes.” Variant B: “Join our upcoming technology workshop.”

Human review: Variant A is more specific, but the project format and 60-minute duration must match the proposed session. Neither variant should imply guaranteed jobs, certificates or outcomes.

Final decision: Use Variant A as the proposed lead message and compare it with Variant B among comparable audiences. Judge by registration conversion, not likes alone.

EXAMPLE 03 · CLAIMS & ETHICS

Reject false certainty

Prompt: “Suggest a strong line for the dashboard summary using the current sample numbers.”

AI output to reject: “The campaign generated 151 registrations at just ₹7.62 each and is on track to hit 500.”

Human review: Those numbers come from illustrative demo rows, not a live campaign. Calling them generated results or claiming the campaign is on track would mislead the reviewer.

Final decision: Label all figures simulated, describe ₹7.62 as a sample cost-per-registration calculation only, and report actual outcomes only after verified data exists.

Final reflection: a suggestion deliberately rejected

I rejected the suggestion to present simulated registrations as campaign results. It could make the proposal sound stronger in the short term, but it would undermine trust and lead to unsupported conclusions. I kept the dashboard useful as a prototype while clearly separating the target, proposed budget, sample data and actual results. Human judgment was necessary to protect accuracy and credibility.
